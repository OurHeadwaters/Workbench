Building a Northwestern Ontario Food Hub Network 
Project Duration: November 1, 2024 - October 31, 2027
Lasted Updated: July 5, 2024 
1.0 Executive Summary 


The Northwestern Ontario Food Hub project aims to revolutionize the regional food system by establishing a collaborative network of farmer-owned hubs throughout the Northwestern Ontario region. With a vision to connect local, sustainable food producers with retail and institutional markets such as grocers, schools, hospitals, and colleges, the project seeks to strengthen the region's economy while promoting access to fresh, locally-grown produce. Led by a dedicated team of community development partners, the project embodies a commitment to fostering resilience and sustainability within the local food ecosystem through sharing supply, services and infrastructure to serve Northwestern Ontario. 

At the heart of the Northwestern Ontario Food Hub project lies a mission to support small family farms by providing them with enhanced market access and opportunities for growth. By leveraging the power of collective action, the project aims to streamline the value chain, enabling farmers to reach a broader customer base while maintaining the integrity of their products. Through this strategic partnership with Ontario Cooperative Association, Superior Seasons, Rock Front Family Farm, Kitchen Sync Strategies and Local Line we will invest in value-added processing, supply chain logistics, and marketing initiatives to empower farmers to thrive in a competitive market landscape..

With a focus on collaboration and innovation, the Northwestern Ontario Food Hub project will serve as a catalyst for positive change within the region's food system. By fostering partnerships between public and private sector stakeholders, the project aims to address systemic challenges and create sustainable solutions that benefit both farmers and consumers alike. Through its commitment to building a resilient and inclusive food system, the project aspires to leave a lasting legacy of economic prosperity and food security for generations to come.

2.0 Project Goals 


Objective 1: Enhance Market Access

The project seeks to provide 40+ small family farms in Northwestern Ontario with expanded market opportunities by connecting them with retail and institutional buyers such as schools, hospitals, and colleges. By facilitating direct relationships between farmers and these large-scale purchasers, the project aims to increase the demand for locally-grown, sustainable food products in Northwestern Ontario. 

Objective #2: Strengthen Economic Resilience

Through strategic investments in value-added processing, supply chain logistics, and marketing initiatives, the project aims to bolster the economic resilience of small-scale agricultural operations in the region. By supporting farmers in diversifying their revenue streams and optimizing their production processes, the project aims to mitigate the impact of external factors such as market fluctuations and climate variability.

Objective #3 Promote Food System Sustainability

The project is committed to advancing principles of sustainability and environmental stewardship within the regional food system. By prioritizing the procurement of locally-grown produce and implementing practices that minimize environmental impact, the project aims to promote ecological resilience and long-term viability within the agricultural sector.

Objective 4: Foster Collaboration and Innovation

Central to the success of the project is the cultivation of collaborative partnerships between public and private sector stakeholders. By fostering an environment of cooperation and knowledge sharing, the project aims to catalyze innovation and create scalable solutions that address systemic challenges within the food system.

Objective 5: Improve Food Access and Equity

By increasing the availability of locally-grown, nutritious food within the region, the project aims to improve food access and equity for all members of the community. Through targeted outreach and education initiatives, the project seeks to empower consumers to make informed food choices and support local farmers.

3.0 Market Analysis 



Industry Overview and Trends
The Northwestern Ontario Food Hub Network is a strategic alliance of regional partners that have identified local Northern businesses who demonstrate a need to expand operations and cultivate new markets to meet regional demand and increase business viability.  


Reports conducted to inform the Northern Ag Strategy have shown that both the shift in consumer preferences and high costs in transportation have resulted in demand for local and regional foods. However, Northern producers, artisans and processors face challenges arising from the small scale of Northern Ontario, resulting in difficulties for local businesses accessing markets successfully. Addressing gaps in infrastructure and services in Northern Ontario such as suppliers, transportation networks and storage, packing, processing and distribution facilities will be essential to increasing market access. The report The Rural and Northern Lens & A Voice for Rural and Northern Ontario discusses the importance of the expansion of the agriculture sector in the north to “build local economic opportunity and simultaneously reduce the need to import foods. This has the potential to lead to increased employment opportunities, while also providing diverse economic opportunities for many rural and northern communities. The network effects that can result from buying local food are very clear.  Many studies show that across local communities in Canada and the US the baseline multiplier effect of buying local food is 1.4-2.6 throughout the wider local economy. A study taking place in Temiskaming found that for every dollar spent at a farm gate, $2.80 to $3.30 is generated in the wider local economy. The impact of this multiplier effect as it compounds can be immense. For example, Sustain Ontario has put forth research that if every household in Ontario spent $10 a week on local food, we would have an estimate of an additional $2.4 billion in our local economy at the end of the year and create 10,000 new jobs. A recent USDA study concluded that of the sales of locally grown food in 2008, $4.8 billion was attributed to farms that sold through only intermediary channels, such as restaurants, food retailers and regional distributors. This is triple the amount of sales sold only through direct to consumer channels.


Reports conducted in our region had shown an imperative for shortening supply chains to meet an increasing market demand for local food.  Replacing non-regional with locally produced food products creates an economic multiplier for these goods, but our region lacks sufficient infrastructure and coordination to ensure local products are sold here instead of products from abroad. Nearly a decade of highly collaborative food system assessments and planning have revealed new public private partnership opportunities and community collaboration to expand mid-tier value chains and increase availability of local foods. This project will implement route optimization, backhaul opportunities, aggregation and cold storage development, strategic region-to-region coordination and application of technical assistance to help producers scale.


Northern Ontario’s agri-food sector primarily consists of small and medium-sized producers, processors and manufacturers. We are facing increasing competition from larger producers and processors in other countries. Growing a More Resilient Supply Chain in Ontario describes several challenges for food entrepreneurs along the supply chain such as processing, managing aggregation, marketing and sales of food. The article suggests facilities such as food hubs are needed in order to help producers reach their customers. In addition, small, rural and remote communities are isolated from the larger clusters and professional networks, limiting their decision-making capacity, their access to investments and their overall competitiveness. Developing networks is critical. 
The Ontario Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs has identified in the Northern Food Strategy report that the development of fully functioning value chains has been a long standing challenge in Northern Ontario. The lack of crucial elements of the value chain has limited Northern Ontario’s ability to strengthen the region's resiliency, increase innovation and implement sustainable strategies for economic and environmental sustainability. Several research projects conducted by the Wallace Center have identified common challenges to creating strong value chains as well as key characteristics of resilient and innovative value chains. Challenges can include lack of ‘hard’ infrastructure’ such as sufficient cold storage capacity or access to regional food hubs with the bandwidth to sell to institutional purchasers. A need for ‘soft infrastructure’ can also be seen such as coordinated networks of producers / growers and artisans who can meet the demand of larger buyers by compiling resources. 

An evaluation of successful value chains reported in the article ‘Values-Based Supply Chains: Supporting Regional Food and Farms’  identified 5 key areas these food hubs and values-aligned distribution systems had in common. These include; access to capital, ability to adhere to regulations and food safety policies, sufficient infrastructure, business acumen among the organizations on the value chain, and sufficient communications and market development.Creating strategies to increase larger buyers sourcing of local food has been associated with increases in local food sales, of over 50% in Alberta, as seen in the 2005 VCI Retail program’s partnership with Save-On Foods and Sunfresh; a local supplier.


Needs Assessment and Gap Analysis
The Northern Food Strategy has identified several gaps in infrastructure in Northern Ontario resulting in stagnation of the local food movement and food insecurity. Some of these include lack of suppliers, transportation networks and storage, packing, processing and distribution facilities which will be essential to increasing market access for small food businesses. The result of these has been higher transportation costs, increased food prices and businesses unable to expand and become profitable. Fledge has identified several reasons why these gaps have not been addressed. Firstly, the resources and infrastructure have not been mapped as extensively in the North as in Southern Ontario. As a result there has been ineffective communication and collaboration between parties who have similar aims and interests. Secondly, Fledge identifies that there is a high cost of doing business in the North and while funding has been provided to Northern communities it has not been focussed on specifically addressing improving transportation infrastructure, processing facilities and logistical support. During 2023, NFDN conducted several Regional Roundtables with producers and support actors within Northern Ontario to gain a deeper understanding of the challenges that they were facing personally or were seeing come across their desk. The consensus of these discussions was that several gaps are present such as: facility access to incubate new northern products, long distances to the nearest dry bins and abattoirs, high transportation costs, minimal available storage and portable freezer space, lack of distribution networks, low availability of processors and limited access to wholesale markets. 


Furthermore, the current infrastructure in place in the North is also under- utilized. There are several organizations acting as aggregators of food to supply to their communities through their brick and mortar businesses that have processing and storage capacity, such as Superior Seasons  in Thunder Bay and the 807 Food Coop in Dryden. Several of the challenges outlined in the Northern Food Strategy Report are applicable here; these communities having limited awareness of the importance of local food for economic sustainability to drive demand, as well as a limited supply of food to these hubs due to their remote location and limited distribution networks. Additionally, transportation companies such as Lock City Dairy are underutilized as a transporter of food between North Western communities. As identified by Forge, the main reason being the lack of coordination taking place between actors in the local food space in Northern Ontario to identify the opportunities available.


The current physical infrastructure in Dryden and Thunder Bay is essential to the sustainability of the local food sector but with dwindling market demand and a lack of staffing to support increasing demand in both communities, we are vulnerable to losing this infrastructure. This infrastructure includes local storefront areas, producer storage, all season growing, cold/frozen storage, commercial kitchens with equipment, office space and a location for community connection for knowledge transfer initiatives & events. 


Without bustling collaborative community spaces working towards enhancing the local food system collaboratively, our community is vulnerable to the centralized food system and in times of crisis, our region will not be able to have the physical infrastructure needed to address concerns as a community, band together and bring food to those that need it. 


Having the shared location to harness waste is critical in Dryden where waste disposal is a growing concern. For example, not only can people come into these areas to shop or pick up orders, 807 Food Co-op offers a point to drop off clean egg cartons, boxes and canning jars, as well as taking the steps to enhance our recovery programs to include compostables supported by the city of dryden and glass recycling. 
 
With the upcoming NWOFHN project, the local food market and networks will expand to offer a collaborative way to tackle regional market challenges as defined in the SCRA. With more feet on the ground to gain exposure for the collaborative project between Dryden, Thunder Bay and Sioux Lookout, not only can inventory sharing and distribution hurdles be addressed in real time, but it will aid in the resiliency of the local food hubs in good times and bad. 


With more directed efforts towards network communications and exposure, the staff at each of the hubs can focus on enhancing our individual physical infrastructure to address specific barriers and community needs. Some examples of projects that are waiting to be initiated when staff capacity grows are improvements to the electrical for indoor growing year round, a noise reduction room for particularly noisy equipment to improve the work environment for hub staff and the addition of an ice machine/freezer for revenue and internal use. 




This project seeks to fill several of these gaps; such as increasing access to wholesale markets, developing viable distribution routes between Northwestern Ontario communities, increasing demand for local food, and providing technical and logistical support to food actors. With the support of Collegea, NFDN, and LFFC conducted a supply chain resilience analysis of Northern Ontario, which identified food actors across NWO, such as farms, food  businesses, institutions, restaurants and more. The research examined population density, average income and current availability of local food to help gauge demand. The SCRA has identified several viable distribution routes to and from Thunder Bay, Dryden and other northern communities such as Sioux Lookout, Kenora and Red Lake. This includes the necessary demand and sales to make these trips viable on a break even basis for the 807 Food Coop and Superior Seasons. By establishing a partnership with distribution partners such as Lock City Dairy or Emily’s Bread  this project seeks to expand the reach of local food enabling products made in Dryden to be sold in several other communities and vice versa with produce grown in Sioux Lookout, Thunder Bay and more. By pooling produce this will additionally enable produce to be sold to institutional markets, a market otherwise unable to be reached by small scale farmers and artisans. With training from industry experts on Value Chain Coordination, such as Kitchen Sync Strategies, 807 seeks to create relationships between institutions and producers to enable this wholesale expansion. 




Impact in Northern Ontario 

The Northwestern Ontario Food Hub project is positioned to deliver substantial economic benefits, with an anticipated revenue of $2.5 million over its duration. By fostering collaboration and cooperation among local producers, processors, store owners, and service providers, the project creates economic opportunities that translate into job creation, increased regional sales, and support for local suppliers. By promoting a sustainable and localized supply chain, the project distinguishes itself from larger industry players and contributes to the reduction of economic leakage. Moreover, its commitment to supporting and growing local businesses not only promotes economic resilience within the region but also preserves the unique identity and quality of locally sourced products. Through these efforts, the project plays a pivotal role in driving economic prosperity and sustainability in Northwestern Ontario, empowering communities and businesses for years to come.


This framework allows our activities to embark on new pathways to support our rural and remote Northern communities. What started years ago in the form of community engagement sessions to explore the impacts, benefits, and opportunities of increased visitation – has expanded into a rich dialogue with community leaders, business owners, and residents working toward “sustainable, resilient and connected food systems” along Northern Ontario’s highway from Thunder Bay to Dryden and Kenora regions. 


Northwestern Ontario miles of rugged, rural coastline consists of Kenora, Rainy River and Thunder Bay. Major communities in the region include Thunder Bay, Kenora, Dryden, Fort Frances, Sioux Lookout, Greenstone, Red Lake, Marathon, and Atikokan. There are also several dozen First Nations- with the largest populated city having 110, 172 residents. Along with a bustling population, Northern Ontario is home to a strong farming and aquaculture industry which has generated over $232 million in gross cash receipts in the 2016 census. Food and beverage processors in Northern Ontario employ more than 1,000 people in 129 locations producing baked goods, beverages, meat and dairy products. However, one of the  hinders to generating local multiplier effects and amplifying this economic development in the region are gaps in accessible  infrastructure.  Local food infrastructure such as processing, aggregation and storage, as well as wholesale buyers are spread sparsely across the region or even located in neighboring regions, creating major inefficiencies for producers – time on the road delivering product means less time on the water or on the farm. Through these efforts, we will also increase health and well being within communities. 


The project will also use a circular food economy approach to a food hub network that involves minimizing waste and maximizing resource utilization within the food supply chain, emphasizing strategies such as waste reduction, resource recovery, sustainable sourcing, shortening supply chains, collaborative partnerships, and innovative technologies. By implementing practices such as composting, organic waste conversion, supporting local farmers, and promoting efficient distribution, this approach aims to create a sustainable, interconnected food system that reduces environmental impact, supports local communities, and addresses challenges related to food security and climate change.

Dryden Facilities 

Located at 60 Princess St., the Dryden Food Hub is home to a 1200 square ft warehouse which has been converted into a custom built commercial kitchen and retail space. Along with the rental, this space includes an 8’ x 10’ cold room, commercial corner sanitizing unit, shelving and custom made hydroponic grow beds. The remaining area is 400 square feet, split into three sections with an office, staff room and storage area. 

Equipment to expand capacity was funded through FEDNOR/RED in the “Dryden Food Hub” project with partner Local Food and Farm Co-op. This includes but is not limited to a chamber vacuum sealer, 4 x stainless steel tables, 2 x display fridges, 3-door display freezer, 4 x deep freezers, ice machine, kitchen aid / attachments, vita mix blender, display tables, electric coolers, pots/pans and other essential kitchen items for seasonal production and knowledge transfer initiatives.  


Sioux Lookout Facilities 

Located at 58 Front Street, Sioux Lookout. This Sioux Lookout facility will be part of an entrepreneurial hub in the downtown core. A former theatre, retail spaces will be leased to the local community futures (PACE). Rock Front Family Farm and the 807 Food Coop will participate in the soon-to-start Business Start-Up program, receiving mentorship and support to grow their connected production and retail spaces in this newly renovated hub.

There will be a dedicated production space of 327 sq./ft for microgreens production and 156 sq./ft retail space for showcasing 807 Food Co-op products. The hub will also include offices for other entrepreneurs and host workshops as well as activities to support entrepreneurial growth within the community.

All facilities are accessible to consumers through their retail spaces in their respective towns. Each facility currently adheres to local zoning requirements and will continue to do so. Clients of 807, Rockfront and Superior Seasons are most likely to shop within their own town, thus acquiring goods from neighboring communities is important to making local food products accessible to each of their customer bases.

Thunder Bay Facilities 
Belluz Farms, located at 752 Candy Mountain Dr, Slate River Valley, ON P7J 0C2, encompasses a diverse range of facilities and sustainable practices. The Thunder Bay facilities boast a rich natural landscape, which includes over 70 acres of woodland, shelterbelts, and grassland, as well as wetland and marsh areas, and 4 ponds. Organic farming methods such as crop rotation, natural pest controls, and soil management are implemented to enhance soil quality, reduce pests, and promote a healthy ecosystem. Moreover, the farm's commitment to providing a positive work environment for its labor force, predominantly students, reflects its dedication to social responsibility and community engagement. 
The greenhouse at Belluz Farms operates with a focus on sustainability and environmental consciousness. Rainwater is collected and recycled through a floor drain system, while organic growing media and compost are utilized to nourish the plants. The farm's use of a wood-chip biomass boiler and an energy retention curtain for heating, alongside biological pest controls and organic fertilizers, underscores its dedication to environmentally friendly practices and organic cultivation. Furthermore, the farm store and Harvest Cafe, though temporarily closed due to Covid, are aligned with eco-friendly principles, offering compostable food ware, biodegradable bag options, and disinfection solutions that leave no harmful residues. In addition to its physical infrastructure, Belluz Farms prioritizes continual improvement and community engagement.
 By investing in professional development and belonging to agricultural organizations and publications, the farm demonstrates its dedication to upholding high standards and best practices within the industry. Furthermore, the farm actively engages with the community through initiatives such as school tours, fundraisers, and partnerships with organizations like the Gleaning Project and local food banks. This multifaceted approach underscores the farm's commitment not only to sustainable agriculture but also to being an active and positive contributor to the local food system and community at large.

7.0 Operational Plan


In partnership with the OCA, 807 Food Co-op develops project-based work plans each year with the targets shared with all relevant staff, and a clear plan of action, deliverables and targets, assignment of responsibilities, and allocation of capital. 

The strategic plan and specifically the operational plan via Rooted Impact is designed to set a solid foundation for the three-year project, ensuring that year one focuses on establishing the necessary groundwork and infrastructure. In year one, Rooted Impact will prioritize activities such as securing resources, building a skilled project team, developing detailed implementation guidelines, and establishing partnerships with key stakeholders. This foundational year will also include setting up robust monitoring and evaluation systems to track progress and outcomes effectively.
As we move into year two, the focus will shift to putting the strategic plan into actionable steps. This involves scaling up project activities, launching key initiatives, and deploying resources to building regional value chains and expanding markets for Northwestern Ontario producers and processors. Year two will emphasize executing the operational plans laid out in the first year, ensuring that project activities are carried out efficiently and effectively. This period will also involve ongoing monitoring and adjustments based on the initial performance data to ensure that the project remains on track and responsive to any emerging challenges or opportunities.
In year three, the project will concentrate on achieving and consolidating its targeted outcomes and impacts. This phase will involve fine-tuning and optimizing operations based on insights gained from the previous years. Efforts will be made to ensure the sustainability of the project’s results, including capacity building for stakeholders and creating mechanisms for ongoing support and maintenance. Throughout this period, comprehensive evaluations will be conducted to assess the overall success of the project, capturing lessons learned and best practices to inform future initiatives. This three-year phased approach ensures that the project is methodically implemented, continuously improved, and ultimately successful in meeting its strategic goals.

This plan is designed to meet the goals and vision established in the three year strategic plan approved by the OCA and 807 Board. Monthly Board meetings, bi-weekly staff meetings, and monthly written staff reports ensure the outcomes meet the plan, or that changes are discussed. The process allows 807 to exceed many required deliverables for Northern Ontario funding, and to leverage secured funding for additional resources for a significant impact on the development of local food systems in the north.

In addition, we have created an integrated and detailed impact measurement spreadsheet of benchmarks to track and evaluate the three years of activities in Northern Ontario and ongoing activities. In addition, we also have evaluation methods from participants for education and training, roundtable discussions and meetings, quarterly check-in reports etc.This approach will be implemented in ongoing work, to create a multi-year report of NWOFHN impact, maintaining coordination between member and stakeholder needs, and to assess the initiatives and strategies of each program area.
