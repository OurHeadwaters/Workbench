Embracing Resilience: Our Journey Since 2020

As I strolled around my yard that spring day in 2020, pushing my baby's stroller and watching my three-year-old frolic in the grass, an unsettling feeling gripped me. The world was in disarray, and I couldn't shake the thought that this disaster might finally awaken people to the need for change.

Rumors of an impending transport driver strike reached my ears. The drivers were facing incredible challenges—they couldn't access public washrooms or find food while on the road, making it impossible for them to carry out their duties effectively.

This situation sparked a series of questions within me. What would happen if the truckers went on strike, considering our region's heavy reliance on "just in time" supplies from big grocery stores? With the disappearance of our local co-op and the potential collapse of farmers' markets, how would those who didn't grow, store, or process their own food fare?

The answers I found were disheartening. Dissatisfied with the possibilities, I firmly believe that if one desires change, one must take responsibility and take action. Thus, I set out to plan a business that would address these concerns.

That momentous decision came in March 2020, and by July, the business was up and running—though not without its challenges. While I hadn't precisely modeled the venture, I understood the urgency of propelling the food system transformation forward.

Since then, an incredible transformation has unfolded in the realm of local food in NWO (Northwestern Ontario), and I'd like to believe that I had a part to play in its progress. Community development aside, my family and I have acquired invaluable skills during these transformative years. We now cultivate microgreens, practice hydroponics, expand our gardens, triple our food preservation efforts, experiment with vegetable fermentation, tend to a lively flock of chickens, embrace freeze-drying techniques, and have mastered an abundance of resourceful techniques to make the most of a gallon of milk and a loaf of bread.

During this journey, I explained to my husband that I was determined to build a "depression business." In a world reminiscent of the 1930s, where making do with what we have becomes essential, preserving food in jars and maximizing resources takes on newfound significance.

Stay tuned as we delve deeper into our experiences, share lessons learned, and explore the incredible resilience that can arise from challenging times. Together, we can navigate the uncharted path ahead, fostering a more sustainable and self-reliant future.

Our projects since 2020

I will never forget the feeling I had that spring day in 2020 while walking my baby around the yard and watching my three year old soar through the grass. 

What was happening in the world? This disaster is finally going to wake people up, I thought to myself. 

I had just heard threats of transport drivers striking. At this point, they weren’t allowed to use public washrooms, go anywhere to get food and they had no way to go about their business effectively. 

What would happen if the truckers went on strike when all we have in our region is the “just in time” supply that the big grocery stores provide. 

We no longer have a co-op connecting farmers and producers, the markets are looking like they won’t be allowed to run…what does that mean for people who don’t grow/store/process their own food? 

I didn’t like the answers to these questions and being a person who believes that if you want something to change, you should take responsibility and take action, I started planning a business. 

That moment happened in March 2020. The business was running by July and although I hadn’t effectively modelled it, I knew that forward motion was essential to get this food system ball rolling. 

So much has happened since then in regards to local food in NWO and I like to believe I had a part to play in the progress that has been made. 

Community development aside, I know myself and my family have gained invaluable skills in these last few years. We now grow microgreens, grow hydroponically, have expanded our gardens, tripled down on our food preservation, we dabble in vegetable fermentation, have some chickens squawking out back, freeze dry everything and know about a million and a half ways to “push forward” a gallon of milk and a loaf of bread.

I told my husband from the start that I’m building a depression business. Working with what you have and sticking things in jars—that’s got 1930 written all over it.

Until recently, I was always “closeted” about my preparedness. People were not interested in engaging in dialogue about a potential economic collapse. I’d try, but blank stares and disinterest proved that they really didn’t care about how money came into our economy, and the catastrophe that would befall when the Ponzi scheme was up. I’ve been talking with my husband since 2011 about this, and although he saw the value in us being prepared, he wasn’t as motivated as I was to ensure we had things lined up while we were still living in times of abundance. 

We still are living in abundance, but I don’t feel as though I need to discuss this in the shadows, people can see that the economy is failing. Technology is taking over as I stated it would, and fuel has been poured on that fire with covid, trending ever so swiftly towards a new normal as it’s been tagged. 

Nobody knows exactly how this will shake out, the banks have their agendas and the powers that be will pull and tug, but there will always be things that come out of left field and chart new courses. 

One thing I know for sure is that I don’t need to stress when I’ve committed my life to the following:

-Building food skills 
-Inspiring community
-Helping others
-Adopting productive hobbies (hunting, fishing, foraging)
-Raising good men who think for themselves and practice empathy

The world is your oyster in good times and bad when you build your life around the simple things that matter. A depression may be around the corner but being depressed never has to be a reality we face.
