Journey in home based business 2024

My home-based business story is unique. Instead of starting with a specific money-making idea, I focused on designing a lifestyle that suits our primarily neurodivergent family through entrepreneurship.

I began by identifying what I didn't want, followed by listing my desired outcomes (add 13 stomps). It became evident that to achieve this lifestyle, I couldn't work for anyone but on a contractual basis.

In March 2020, with no prior experience other than being a consumer, I ventured into the local food industry. My background in Recreation Management and Community Development from UofM, coupled with a passion for grassroots development, set the foundation for my leap.

The tumultuous year of 2020 emphasized the importance of community building and collaboration in reshaping the economy. Recognizing the significance of food in people's budgets, the rising prices, and the essential skill of food production amidst uncertainties, we began sourcing farm-fresh produce and packaging it creatively.

Through our journey, we delved into growing microgreens, exploring kratky hydroponics, forming alliances with local producers, and eventually establishing a cooperative food hub for sustainable food system development.

Our food hub addressed the challenges faced by individuals unable or unwilling to license their home kitchens for food production. With a household filled with pets and hobbies, the concept of food handling at home didn't align with our values. Many shared this sentiment, prompting the creation of a safe and collaborative space for culinary endeavors.

Reflecting on the flaws of Dryden’s previous cooperative, I delved into extensive research to mitigate these issues through a private business approach to filling the gaps. Establishing the necessary infrastructure, I set out to facilitate distribution to and from Thunder Bay, understanding that these solutions were not definitive but a stepping stone towards collaboration and progress.

Our efforts culminated in the creation of the 807 Food Co-op, specifically designed to address local food challenges in a non-profit capacity.

While my business is labeled "home-based," it thrives with the support of the 807 Food Hub. Membership in the hub offers me valuable resources to efficiently manage my remote home business while homeschooling my children.

Key benefits include:
- Access to a staffed Dryden location during business hours for shipping and receiving
-Cross marketing and sales opportunities
-One production day per week in the kitchen
- Convenient storage for production ingredients
- Space for workshops, content creation, and seasonal production
- A designated Food Premise address for labeling compliance
- Shared equipment to keep large items out of my home and use for special events
- A well-equipped kitchen with ample space and amenities
- A consistent supply of local produce and goods

Securing this dedicated space is crucial for my operations, and the cooperative model proves to be a sustainable solution, fostering growth for new production and home-based ventures.

DRAFT 

My story is different from your typical home based business. I didn’t have a specific idea I thought was going to take in the cash, I just knew that being in business is the way that I could  design my lifestyle in the way that suits our family, who are primarily neurodivergent. 

I made a list of what I don’t want. 
Then I list of what I do want.
Add 13 stomps.

It was pretty clear that in order to live that life, I couldn’t work for someone else unless it’s contractual. 

It was March 2020 and I decided to take a leap into the local food game without a shred of experience beyond as a consumer. 

I went to UofM for Recreation Management and Community Development, and always had a place in my heart for grassroots development models. Historically, in times of societal upheaval (which 2020 was one for the history books) building community, production & businesses in collaboration with each other can profoundly change the course of the economy when conditions stabilize. It gives a huge leg up to communities to build when others are buried in their phones. 

Knowing the biggest part of peoples’ budget is food, and that prices will continue to rise beyond the cost of wages, and that we have a declining producer population, and that I have a homestead, and that it’s a skill I want to pass on to my kids, and that knowing how to grow and store food is  essential in uncertain times…We just started buying farm fresh food and dolling it up in these crates with jars. 

We did that while learning how to grow micro greens, learning kratky hydroponics, making connections with local food producers, and eventually building a local food hub for a co-operative model of food system development.

So this food hub was built because many of us can’t (or don’t want to) license their home kitchen for food production. We have a lot of pets and hobbies, so the idea of food handling at our home didn’t sit right with us. A lot of people run water off a well, which is a regulatory hurdle, along with many others. 

The last co-operative in town had flaws, so I went through all the research to attempt to address the problems with private business. I made the infrastructure and began pursuing distribution to and from Thunder Bay. I didn’t expect any of these solutions to be the be all end all, but I knew if I just started the cart rolling, it would be much easier to start collaborating with like minds and finding the right path. 

Which is what happened. We built 807 Food Co-op to efficiently work through the local food hurdles that we are facing in a not for profit way. 

Although my business is “home-based”, it is also supported by the 807 Food Hub. As a hub member, I can access some perks that help me efficiently run a home business in the bush alongside homeschooling my children.

Some of the perks that work for me are:

-a place in Dryden with business hours to have shipping/receiving 
-a place that I can come and go to store ingredients for my production day
-a place that I can lead workshops and do content creation
-an address with a Food Premise number that I can put on my food labels 
-shared equipment and a space to keep big equipment out of my home 
-a kitchen that is never ransacked by children and has ample counter space, sinks and a sanitizer. 
-a way that we can keep a stock of our favourite local foods

Having the space is so essential but more than I can justify for my business alone, so the co-op model just made more sense to empower new production and home businesses.
