# Codetry Test 010 — Water-register wordings on the shortfall CTA

**Date:** 2026-04-27
**Zone:** 1 — Headwaters / xBuckets (daily kitchen garden)
**App:** xbuckets PWA, Payday tab → wallet-confirm sheet
**Tester:** Bobbie + Replit Agent
**Surface tested:** mockup canvas, shape `water-register-shortfall-cta`
**File:** `artifacts/mockup-sandbox/src/components/mockups/payday-rain-test/WaterRegisterShortfallCta.tsx`

---

## The question (one line)

Test 009 graduated the *demoted* XRP-swap chip from "Convert XRP → RLUSD" to
**"Top up reservoir from XRP"**, finally bringing it into the locked Zone 1
water dialect (test 002). That chip has a *loud* twin — the prominent amber
CTA in the wallet-confirm sheet that appears when the wallet is short of
plan total. It still says **"Convert XRP → RLUSD to cover shortfall"**.
Same action, same destination, same protocol-noun register-break. This test
asks: can the loud chip speak the same dialect as its quiet sibling without
losing the urgency the amber card is built to express?

## What was on screen

The canvas surface put four wordings in the same slot — the amber CTA at
the bottom of a faithful render of the production shortfall card (white
amber-bordered card with: Wallet balance row, Plan total row, an amber
alert reading *"Wallet is $420 short. Buckets will be filled
proportionally to your actual $780 — each bucket gets its fair scaled-down
share."*). The card is the surrounding context that defines the CTA's
register: the sheet is shouting *act* in amber, and the CTA is the action.

- **Variant 0 (control)** — production wording.
  "Convert XRP → RLUSD to cover shortfall."
  Mechanical wallet/swap language with explicit urgency tail. Speaks raw
  asset/protocol nouns — the only register-break left in the
  wallet-confirm sheet after test 009 fixed the demoted chip.
- **Variant A — direct port of test 009's graduate.**
  "Top up reservoir from XRP."
  Same exact wording as the demoted chip after test 009. Dialect-correct,
  but drops the word *shortfall* — and the shortfall is the entire reason
  this CTA exists on this surface. The user did not request a swap; the
  user is being offered a swap to fix a problem the surface is calling
  out.
- **Variant B — 009's graduate + urgency tail.**
  "Top up reservoir from XRP · cover the shortfall."
  Test 009's graduated phrase as the action, then a separator, then the
  urgency tail. Echoes the demoted chip exactly in the first half (so the
  two siblings rhyme), then names the shortfall the surrounding amber card
  is shouting about.
- **Variant C — refill + paired tail.**
  "Refill reservoir from XRP · cover the shortfall."
  Same shape as B but with *refill* instead of *top up*. Rhythmically
  tighter, but *refill* carries an implication that the reservoir was once
  full and is now empty — slightly off here, since the reservoir is
  partially full ($780 of $1,200) and never was full to begin with this
  pay cycle.

The test reads each chip cold, with the amber alert "Wallet is $420 short"
sitting right above it, and asks whether the cold reader can still tell
what the chip *does* — and whether the urgency the amber card is built to
express is still on the chip.

## Falsifiable claim

A water-register wording earns the loud amber CTA if **both** of these
hold:

1. **Mechanical clarity preserved.** A cold reader, with no prior context,
   can still tell from the chip alone what the chip does — specifically,
   that XRP gets exchanged for stablecoins added to the wallet. (Same
   falsifier as tests 003 and 009.)
2. **Urgency preserved.** The cold reader still understands this is a
   *fix-the-shortfall* action, not a casual top-up. The chip should read
   as the answer to the amber alert above it, not as an aside that
   happens to live in the same card.

It fails if it loses mechanical precision (a cold reader can't tell what
tapping it does), or if dropping/burying *shortfall* makes the CTA feel
like an afterthought instead of the answer to the amber alert.

## What happened, read cold

- **Variant 0** is mechanically clear and the urgency is explicit
  (*to cover shortfall* nailed onto the end). But it is now the *only*
  register-break left in the wallet-confirm sheet — every other word in
  the card sits inside the locked Zone 1 word map (test 002), and the
  *demoted* twin chip on the same screen (Payday tab) was just graduated
  to the water dialect in test 009. Variant 0 is fine in isolation; on
  the surface it shares with its newly-graduated sibling, it now sounds
  like the only person in the room speaking a different language.
- **Variant A** is the cleanest port — *exactly* what test 009 graduated.
  But it strips the word *shortfall*, and the shortfall is the entire
  reason this CTA exists on this surface. The amber card above the chip
  spends three lines explaining there is a $420 shortfall; the chip
  answering it should reference that explanation, not pretend it isn't
  there. A cold reader sees "Wallet is $420 short… *Top up reservoir
  from XRP*" and the connection holds (top up = fix the short), but it's
  inferred rather than stated. The amber treatment is doing more work
  than the words. Falsifier #2 bites: the urgency is in the card, not in
  the chip.
- **Variant B** is the strongest. The first half ("Top up reservoir from
  XRP") rhymes with the demoted chip exactly — same verb, same object,
  same source — so a user who has seen the demoted chip on the Payday
  tab recognises this as its loud sibling instantly. The dot-separator
  tail ("· cover the shortfall") names what the amber card is calling
  out, in plain dialect-neutral English (*shortfall* is the same word
  the alert uses one line above). Mechanical precision: held. Urgency:
  held — the tail makes the chip the answer to the alert, not an aside.
  The composition (*action · purpose*) mirrors the existing graduated
  hero CTA from test 001 ("Channel the rainfall · $1,200 ready · every
  drop into a bucket"), so it sits inside an existing pattern.
- **Variant C** is rhythmically the tightest of the three (*refill* is
  one syllable shorter than *top up*, and the parallel structure is
  cleaner). But *refill* implies the reservoir was full and is now
  empty — which is slightly wrong for this surface, where the reservoir
  is partially full ($780 of $1,200) and was never full to begin with
  this pay cycle. The same word survived test 009 only as a rejected
  variant (Variant C in test 009, "refill reservoir") — and 009 rejected
  it for losing mechanical precision (no source). Adding "from XRP" here
  fixes the precision issue, but the *fullness* implication remains.
  C is *almost* B, with one wrong-temperature word.

Bobbie's reaction in plain words: *"Variant B is the obvious one — it's
009's chip with its urgency hat on. A is too quiet for amber, C is
beautiful but says the wrong thing about the reservoir's state, 0 is the
last register-break in the room."*

## The rule held (and one quiet finding)

Test 009 named the rule: *one register per screen* applies even to a
typographically demoted surface. Test 010 extends it: it also applies even
to a *loud* surface — the amber CTA was hiding behind its urgency the way
the demoted chip was hiding behind its typography. The amber treatment
draws the eye, but the words still have to sit inside the locked dialect.

The quiet finding in this test: when a chip has a *quiet* sibling and a
*loud* sibling that do the same thing, the loud one should rhyme with the
quiet one in dialect, then add whatever the loud surface needs. Variant
B's structure — *(009's exact graduate) · (purpose-of-this-surface)* —
gives the two chips a shared spine, so the user reads the loud one as
*"oh, the same chip, with the reason"*. This is not a new general rule,
just a working pattern for chips that exist in both quiet and loud forms.

## Verdict

**Variant B graduates.** The production wording moves from
"Convert XRP → RLUSD to cover shortfall" to
**"Top up reservoir from XRP · cover the shortfall"** — same amber CTA
treatment, same surface position, same icon. The chip remains a loud
amber call-to-action; it now also speaks the locked Zone 1 dialect, and
its first half rhymes exactly with the demoted twin chip graduated in
test 009.

Variants A and C are preserved on the canvas as counter-tests:
- A was rejected for dropping *shortfall* — leaving the urgency in the
  amber card alone, with no echo on the chip.
- C was rejected for *refill* implying a state of fullness the reservoir
  never had this cycle — same word, same problem 009 already flagged on
  the demoted chip, on a slightly different surface.

## What changed in production

- `artifacts/xbuckets/src/copy.en.ts` — added
  `convertXrp.shortfallCta: "Top up reservoir from XRP · cover the shortfall"`.
- `artifacts/xbuckets/src/copy.fr.ts` — added FR parity
  `convertXrp.shortfallCta: "Remplir le réservoir depuis XRP · combler le manque"`.
- `artifacts/xbuckets/src/pages/PaydayPlanner.tsx` (~line 2905) — chip
  wording switched from a hardcoded `"Convert XRP → RLUSD to cover shortfall"`
  string to `{COPY.convertXrp.shortfallCta}`. As a side-effect of the
  graduation, the chip is now locale-aware (it had been hardcoded English
  before, showing English to FR users — the same small pre-existing
  register-break that 009 fixed in passing on the demoted chip).

## What would change the verdict

- If telemetry shows users no longer recognise the loud chip as a swap
  affordance (drop in tap rate, increase in confused support questions
  from FR users, increase in "I don't have any reservoir" replies), the
  fallback is *not* Variant 0 but a small variant test at amber-CTA
  scale — the lesson from 009 is that the dialect is teachable, the
  question is just whether the surface taught it well enough.
- If a future task moves the swap UI itself out of XRP-as-protocol
  language into the bamboo-field/headwaters dialect (per test 003's
  *"What would change the verdict"* clause 1 — *a bamboo-only surface*),
  the sheet header would graduate too. That is its own test, not this
  one.
