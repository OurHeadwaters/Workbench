# Codetry Test 010 — Water-register wording on the swap-sheet header

**Date:** 2026-04-27
**Zone:** 1 — Headwaters / xBuckets (daily kitchen garden)
**App:** xbuckets PWA, Payday tab → ConvertXrpSheet
**Tester:** Bobbie + Replit Agent
**Surface tested:** mockup canvas, shape `swap-sheet-header`
**File:** `artifacts/mockup-sandbox/src/components/mockups/payday-rain-test/SwapSheetHeader.tsx`

---

## The question (one line)

Test 009 graduated the wallet/XRP-swap chip into the locked Zone 1 dialect
("Top up reservoir from XRP"), and explicitly *deferred* the same question
one surface deeper — the sheet that opens when the user taps the chip.
This test asks whether `convertXrp.title` should follow the chip into the
locked dialect, or earn its protocol nouns because the user has crossed
into a transactional room.

## Why this needed its own test

Test 009 left the *inside* of the sheet alone — `convertXrp.title` still
reads "Convert XRP → RLUSD" — because once the user has tapped through
the chip they are inside the actual swap interface where protocol nouns
("XRP", "RLUSD") carry weight that "reservoir" alone does not. The
defence in 009 was specific:

> "the swap interface is allowed its own register because by then the
> user has crossed into a different room."

That is defensible and it might be right. But it was an agent decision
made in passing, with no falsifier and no cold read. It deserves its
own test, with both opposite hypotheses run cold.

## What was on screen

The canvas surface put four sheet-header wordings in the same slot,
beneath a chip-tap → sheet-open transition thumbnail (so the cold reader
sees both surfaces in sequence, the way a user actually experiences the
register handoff). The eyebrow was held constant across all four
variants — "Tap the Headwaters", already in dialect, locked by test 002 —
because only the title is the question under test. Two body line-items
("XRP balance · 412 XRP", "RLUSD to receive · ~$420") and the CTA
("Convert in Xaman") were also held constant under each variant so the
cold reader could see that **the protocol nouns are present in the body
regardless of how the title is worded** — they're just no longer the
headline.

- **Variant 0 (control)** — production wording.
  Eyebrow: "Tap the Headwaters" (dialect). Title: **"Convert XRP →
  RLUSD"** (protocol). The internal register-break inside the sheet
  head — eyebrow speaks one language, title speaks another.
- **Variant A — top up your reservoir.**
  Title: **"Top up your reservoir"**. Pure dialect. Mirrors the chip's
  graduated verb ("Top up reservoir from XRP", test 009). The room
  speaks one language end-to-end.
- **Variant B — refill the reservoir from your headwaters.**
  Title: **"Refill the reservoir from your headwaters"**. Both locked
  nouns from the test 002 map. Most poetic of the four. Most prone to
  redundancy with the eyebrow, which already says "Headwaters".
- **Variant C — hybrid.**
  Title: **"Top up your reservoir"** with a small grey subheading
  **"XRP → RLUSD"** directly underneath. Tries to give both hypotheses
  what they want — dialect carries the headline, protocol nouns sit
  demoted as a one-line subheading.

## Falsifiable claim

A water-register sheet title earns the surface if **both** of these hold:

1. **Transactional clarity preserved.** A cold reader, having tapped the
   chip on the Payday tab, can still tell from the sheet header alone
   that this is the XRP-to-RLUSD swap they just opened — without having
   to scroll to the line-items to confirm.
2. **Honest at sign-time.** The title sits inside the locked dialect
   without making the moment of signing feel evasive about which assets
   are moving. Protocol nouns are still present on the surface — in the
   line-items below the title and on the CTA — so dialect at the title
   level is *demotion of mechanics*, not *withholding* of them.

It fails if it loses transactional clarity at the headline level (cold
reader can't tell what the sheet is for), or if the dialect feels like
it is hiding the swap behind a metaphor (the "evasive at the moment
honesty matters most" failure mode hypothesis 2 warns about).

## What happened, read cold

- **Variant 0** is fine read in isolation but reads as a *stutter*
  read in sequence with the chip. The chip says "Top up reservoir from
  XRP" (dialect verb, locked noun, source asset). You tap. The sheet
  that opens calls itself "Convert XRP → RLUSD" (protocol verb, two
  protocol nouns). The eyebrow above the title — "Tap the Headwaters"
  — is also in dialect, so the title is doubly isolated: it is the only
  protocol-language unit inside a fully-dialect frame. It hides in
  production today the same way the chip's old wording hid in test 009:
  by being the first thing your eye lands on after a frame that sets
  the register, the register-break is harder to notice than it ought
  to be.
- **Variant A** is the strongest. Read cold after the chip-tap, the
  chip says "Top up reservoir from XRP" and the sheet that opens
  immediately confirms "Top up your reservoir." That is not a stutter;
  it is a *handoff*. The "your" personalises the action — this is now
  *your* reservoir specifically — at the moment the user is about to
  authorise a transfer into it. The protocol nouns have not gone
  anywhere: they sit immediately under the title in the body
  ("XRP balance · 412 XRP", "RLUSD to receive · ~$420") and on the
  CTA ("Convert in Xaman"). At the headline level, dialect; at the
  line-item level, protocol; at the action button, both. Each register
  is doing the job it is best at: dialect names *what kind of action*
  this is, protocol names *what assets* the action moves, and the
  CTA names *the verb the user signs in their wallet*.
- **Variant B** is correct in dialect but stutters with its own
  eyebrow. The eyebrow says "Tap the Headwaters" and the title then
  says "Refill the reservoir from your headwaters" — two uses of
  "headwaters" inside ten words of each other. The surface starts
  to read like a poem rather than a sheet header. There is also a
  quieter problem: at sign-time, *headwaters → XRP* is a mapping the
  user has to recall (test 002), whereas *reservoir → wallet* is more
  immediate because the wallet is what they are about to credit. Both
  mappings are locked, but only one is needed at the title level —
  the *destination* one. Variant A uses just the destination noun;
  Variant B uses both, and the source noun fights with the eyebrow.
- **Variant C** is the most clever and the one that loses the cold
  read. The hybrid asks the surface to do two register-jobs at once at
  the title level — dialect headline plus protocol subheading — when
  the rest of the sheet is already organised to do exactly that
  separation across the title and the body. Putting "XRP → RLUSD" as
  a small grey subheading directly under the title makes the protocol
  nouns *quiet but adjacent to the headline*, which is the exact shape
  test 009 rejected for the chip ("it just makes it longer"). The body
  line-items already do this work better, with more space and more
  context. C's only real argument is that it's a safe-harbour
  compromise. But codetry tests are not for safe-harbour compromises;
  they are for finding which hypothesis the surface actually wants.

Bobbie's reaction in plain words: *"Variant A is the chip's promise
kept. The chip says 'top up reservoir' and the sheet says 'top up your
reservoir' — same room, one beat later, with 'your' added because now
it's about you. Variant 0 isn't wrong, but you can feel the gear
change. Variant B says 'headwaters' twice. Variant C is trying to
have it both ways and the sheet body already does that better."*

## The rule held (and one quiet finding)

Test 002's locked word map says **Reservoir = the stablecoin wallet**.
Test 009 honoured that mapping on the chip (one surface up). This test
honours it on the next surface down — the sheet header — and confirms
that the dialect can carry a transactional headline without becoming
evasive about what the user is signing, *as long as* the protocol nouns
are still present on the surface (in the line-items and on the CTA) to
do the asset-naming work the title no longer does.

The quiet finding in this test: **register zoning works vertically as
well as horizontally.** Test 002 zones registers across screens (Zone 1
speaks water; Zone 2 speaks lineage). This test shows the same kind of
zoning *inside* a single component: the title-level register can speak
dialect while the line-item register speaks protocol, as long as each
register lives at the level it is best at. Dialect names the *kind of
action*; protocol names the *assets the action moves*; the CTA verb
names *what the wallet will sign*. Three registers, three jobs, one
sheet, no register-break.

This is not a new rule — it is test 002's locked map applied with one
extra grain of resolution: not just *which words* per screen, but
*which words at which level inside a screen*. Worth naming because it
unblocks a class of cases (transactional sheets with poetic frames)
where hypothesis 2's "the moment of honesty needs protocol" fear was
load-bearing for the conservative call in 009.

## Verdict

**Variant A graduates.** The production wording moves from
**"Convert XRP → RLUSD"** to **"Top up your reservoir"**. Same eyebrow
("Tap the Headwaters"), same body line-items, same CTA. The sheet
header now matches the chip dialect end-to-end while the protocol
nouns continue to do their job in the body and on the CTA where they
belong.

Hypothesis 2 (the sheet earns its protocol nouns because the user is
crossing into a transactional room) loses on the cold read because
the rest of the sheet *already* names the assets — XRP balance,
RLUSD to receive, slippage in XRP, "Convert in Xaman" on the CTA.
Withholding XRP/RLUSD from the title does not withhold them from the
surface; it just reassigns which register lives at the title level.

Variants B and C are preserved on the canvas as counter-tests:

- B was rejected for stuttering with its own eyebrow ("Headwaters"
  appears twice inside ten words) and for using the source noun at
  the headline level when only the destination noun is needed there.
- C was rejected for replicating, badly, the title/body register
  separation the sheet already does well — turning the title into
  two lines when the body line-items already carry the protocol nouns
  with more space and more context.

## What changed in production

- `artifacts/xbuckets/src/copy.en.ts` — `convertXrp.title` changed
  from `"Convert XRP → RLUSD"` to `"Top up your reservoir"`.
- `artifacts/xbuckets/src/copy.fr.ts` — `convertXrp.title` changed
  from `"Convertir XRP → RLUSD"` to `"Remplissez votre réservoir"`
  (FR parity, same dialect verb as the chip's `chipEntry`
  "Remplir le réservoir depuis XRP").

The eyebrow (`convertXrp.sectionLabel`) is unchanged — it was already
in dialect ("Tap the Headwaters" / "Puisez à la source").

The body line-item labels (`xrpBalanceLabel`, `rlusdToReceiveLabel`,
`xrpCostLabel`, etc.) are unchanged — they correctly use protocol
nouns at the line-item level, which is precisely the register zoning
this test confirmed. The CTA buttons (`convertInXaman`, `convertViaWc`)
are unchanged — they correctly use the protocol verb at the
sign-the-transaction moment.

The headwaters caution (`headwatersCautionTitle`, `headwatersCautionBody`)
is unchanged — it already speaks dialect ("You're tapping the
headwaters") and was always in register.

`ConvertXrpSheet.tsx` itself does not change: it reads `C.title` and
`C.sectionLabel` from the COPY object as before. The graduation is a
pure copy change, no component refactor needed.

## What would change the verdict

- If telemetry shows users hesitating in the sheet (longer dwell on
  the title, more cancels at the title view, more re-reads of the
  body line-items before tapping Convert) compared to the
  "Convert XRP → RLUSD" baseline, hypothesis 2 wins after all and
  Variant 0 returns. The hypothesis here is that the body line-items
  and the CTA carry the asset-naming work well enough that the title
  is freed to speak the room's dialect — but the only honest test of
  that is production usage.
- If a future Zone 1 word-map revision retires "reservoir" as the
  noun for the stablecoin wallet (it won't, but if), the title would
  graduate to whatever the new locked word becomes, and the chip
  would graduate alongside it. The two surfaces are now wired in
  series.
