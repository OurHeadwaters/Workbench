What it communicates, in this order:

1. The mechanism (lead with this — no preamble)
Show the data flow as a simple visual diagram or clearly structured step list:

Document (practitioner-issued record)
  → SHA-256 hash
  → XRPL transaction memo field
  → Immutable ledger entry
  → Verifiable by anyone without trusting Headwaters
The record belongs to the person. The hash is the witness. The ledger is the notary. Headwaters is not in the verification path.

2. The trust model (3–4 sentences)
No central authority holds the record. Verification requires only the original document, its hash, and the public XRPL transaction — all independently checkable. The person controls who holds the plaintext. The ledger only ever sees the hash.

3. What it carries vs. what it is not (two columns)

What the ledger carries:

Document hashes anchored in memo fields
DID fragments — identity records owned by the person
Practitioner stamps — named, witnessed lifecycle crossings
Learning handoffs — student-owned school-year records
What it is not:

Not a token or financial instrument
Not a DeFi protocol or asset issuance layer
Not a speculative position on XRP price
Not in scope for payment system regulation — the Clarity Act's reach into identity layers is an open question we're watching
4. The governance dependency (one callout block)
"Who controls the default validator list controls the notary. The dUNL's integrity is an infrastructure dependency for this build — not a regulatory question, an architectural one. Validator diversity is not optional when the ledger is load-bearing infrastructure for identity records."

The Founding Drift DID: a community identity record anchored at genesis
The Threshold App: lifecycle crossing records — birth, school transitions, care transitions — hashed and witnessed
The Learning Identity Architecture: student-owned learning records with teacher handoffs at the XRPL/DID layer
