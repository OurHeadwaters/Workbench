---

You don't understand a food system by reading about it. You understand it by watching who eats and who doesn't, who owns the till and who doesn't, where the margin goes at the end of the day.

Here is what you see when you watch long enough.

---

One store. No other choice.

Most of the federal grocery help never reaches the shelf.

A family of four spends $1,680 a month on groceries in a northern community. The same basket costs $1,000 down south. Of every federal grocery help dollar that flows in, only 58¢ reaches the shelf. The store keeps the rest. Between $1.6 and $2.0 million in grocery spend leaves the community every year — to Winnipeg, or to the one store in town.

These are not projections. They are the numbers a practitioner finds when she sits down and does the math on a specific community.

---

Community ownership changes one number, and that number changes everything.

The band owns it. René hires the couple and runs the floor, with locals pitching in. With a community-owned store, the number that was 58¢ climbs to 84¢. The other 26¢ doesn't disappear — it shows up as lower prices, paid jobs, and a board the community sits on.

That is not a talking point. It is arithmetic.

---

The store has to keep working when people don't show up. That is not a design preference — it is a northern reality. Hunting season comes. Bad weather comes. Somebody can't make it in. The store opens anyway, or it doesn't.

Here is the answer that works at this scale:

Two people on the cockpit. Software underneath. Payroll stays small. Doors stay open when somebody can't make it in. Truck arrives loaded. Margin comes home fast.

Chris and Morgan on the cockpit — brought in and paid by the contractor, same setup as the band's hotel. Two on payroll, not a row of managers. Only way the math works at this size.

Serious software underneath them: Square at the till, QuickBooks on the books, Local Line for producers, the Headwaters cockpit tying them together. Doors stay open through hunting season and bad weather. Truck leaves Dryden loaded even when one operator is out.

Margin comes home in year one. About $125,000–$200,000 of grocery margin stays in the community the first year — money that today flies south at 58¢ on the dollar.[^1] Four full-time roles — two contractor, two Headwaters — plus a band casual pool of fifteen or more people getting paid hours each week.

---

The contract that holds this together is not complicated. Three line items. One team that gets it done.

**Line 1 — The store's operating system.** The cashier's screen, the daily close, the public price page, the household lookup — built once, owned by the band.

**Line 2 — The way the food gets here.** Three lanes: truck on the road, winter-road truck, plane. Planned route by route. Tested before the store opens.

**Line 3 — The people, trained.** Everyone learns every job. The band runs from a written guide, not from memory. A community board sits over the work.

Two people run the floor. Square, QuickBooks, and Local Line do the back end. Doors stay open, truck stays on time, money comes home fast.

---

The cockpit makes three promises to the community and to the contractor alike.

**A system, not a person.** The same screens survive every retirement. Same till. Same books. Same producer cycle.

**A practice you can sell.** Built on Square, QuickBooks, and Local Line — tools the next contractor already knows.

**A handover, on purpose.** What's only in your head right now becomes the operating manual the buyer pays for.

---

This is the work. Now here is how the work is done.

Every engagement follows the same six phases, in order, with open numbers at every stage.

**Discovery.** Community interviews, existing assets audit, site assessment. What the band already has and what is missing.

**Supply chain mapping.** 807 supplier directory built from scratch. Freight routing, seasonal availability windows, minimum order realities. No assumptions about what comes from the south.

**Staffing and training plan.** Local hire plan, role definitions written in plain language, 30-day training timeline. Built so operators can run it without a consultant in the room.

**Financing structure.** Band council financing options, grant matching against the 807 grants index, co-op structure options with open financial model. Every number is visible and editable.

**Operations manual.** Day-one procedures, daily close, weekly inventory cycle, monthly reconciliation. Written for the person doing the job, not for the person who hired the consultant.

**Handoff.** Band council presentation. Operator walkthrough. Everything handed off in a format the community owns — no login required, no ongoing relationship required.

---

It is worth saying what this plan is not.

Not a report that sits on a shelf.
Not a proposal that needs another proposal to proceed.
Not a template from a southern consulting firm.
Not contingent on an ongoing retainer to keep working.

---

The platform follows governance — not the other way around.

Read your bylaws, map your member categories, identify what needs to be digital and what should stay paper. Then build. Built in the open — you can see it working at every stage.

The tools that emerge from that process are built around how your operation actually works. Changed when your operation changes.

---

The same principle holds in other rooms.

In a residential care home, the clipboard doesn't know what a good shift looks like. The staff already do. They have always known. The problem is the tool — the clipboard, the shared Excel file, the sticky notes on the nursing station — has never matched what the staff actually do.

Bright Side replaces the clipboard, the shared Excel file, and the sticky notes on the nursing station. It is a mobile-first staff tool for residential care — built so an untrained person can pick it up mid-shift and not break anything. It doesn't ask staff to change how they work. It asks them to record what they're already doing, in the language they already use.

---

Here is how the builder describes this tool, and the disclosure is worth including in full:

I built this because I've watched care home staff work around tools that weren't made for them. A 30-minute call is enough to show you what it does and whether it fits your floor. No demo environment, no slide deck — I'll walk you through the actual tool on an actual device.

No obligation. If it's not the right fit, I'll say so.

---

Across all of this work — the store plan, the co-op platform, the care home tool — the way an engagement begins is the same.

Reply to this document with the first task or question you'd like work started on. That's the start date. No contract ceremony required — a written reply counts as authorization to begin.

Either party may pause or end the engagement with two weeks' written notice. All work product and digital assets produced remain the property of the community.

---

Now look at the practitioner's own economics.

One practitioner, working in sequence, with a clear map of what generates margin and what doesn't.

**Community contracts** are the bridge. Active relationships, clear scope, money in motion. This is the work that funds time to build everything else. Do not let the longer-horizon thinking crowd out the next billable hour.

**Salts and syrup** — Parr's Jars — is the only cash-positive stream with no debt load. The June farmers market is a forcing function: production must be confirmed before the first stall date, not after. And the market is not just a product channel. It is the highest-density local audience you'll have all season. Have a one-liner ready for what you do beyond jars.

**Start9 privacy servers** are low friction to start, no compliance path required, cash on delivery. Every Start9 buyer is a warm consulting lead. Treat setup calls as discovery sessions, not transactions.

---

And underneath all of it, the practitioner's own discipline — the one that makes the arithmetic work:

Where every dollar goes, in plain English.

Debt-attack mode: draw only $4,000 a month from the business. The rest is forgone and stays as business surplus — all of which goes to debt. The tithe, $400, is the first claim on the draw. Take-home is $3,600.

This is not austerity for its own sake. It is the posture that makes every other number in this chapter possible — the community margin that stays local, the system that doesn't depend on a single person, the handover that happens on purpose rather than by accident.

You watch the beavers build the dam because you are building one too.
