## PT3.1 — Practice: The first act

The first challenge was not to find a client. It was not to write a plan. It was not to secure a contract or identify a community in need.

The first challenge was to turn the home from a liability into an asset. With just one thing: practice.

Not money. Not expertise borrowed from a course. Not a credential that grants the right to enter. Practice — the slow, daily, repeatable act of doing the work on the ground where you already live.

The home is Zone 0. The practitioner does not begin in a community. The practitioner begins in the household. The garden is planted before the field extension is designed. The books are kept before the community ledger is built. The jar goes on the shelf before the Seasonal Shelf is named. The well pump is installed before the water system is mapped. The heating system is understood before the community energy plan is drawn.

This is not metaphor. It is sequence.

A practitioner who has not run the rename test on their own household vocabulary cannot run it honestly on a community's. A practitioner who has not held a load-bearing noun under pressure at home cannot hold one under pressure at a council meeting. The standing to speak is earned before the speaking begins, and it is earned in the place where the consequences are personal.

Zone 0 is not a stage the practitioner moves through. It is the ground the practitioner stands on for the whole arc of the work. The community engagement deepens the practice; it does not replace it. The harvest still happens. The jars still go up. The accounts are still kept.

The practitioner who lets Zone 0 atrophy in order to serve Zone 3 has abandoned the source of the standing that made the service possible.

## PT3.2 — The host

The practitioner requires a host.

Not a client. Not a contact. Not a procurement officer or a band councillor who agreed to take a meeting. A host — someone who has opened their door and meant it. Someone whose table the practitioner sits at, whose food the practitioner eats, whose stories the practitioner receives before a single proposal is put on paper.

The entry point is a kitchen table. Not a boardroom. Not a Zoom call. Not a formal presentation in the band office with slides up on the wall. A kitchen table, with food on it that the host caught or grew or preserved, and the story of how it got there.

The fish on the plate came from a specific lake, caught on a specific morning, with a specific method the family has used for three generations. The fish story is not small talk. It is the first data. It tells the practitioner what the community knows about its own food system that will never appear in a grant report or a municipal planning document. The fish story is where the load-bearing nouns live, before anyone has been asked to produce them.

A listening ear. A keen eye. The grounding data.

These three arrive in that order. The listening comes first, because the practitioner has no standing to observe until they have demonstrated that they can be still. The keen eye follows, because observation without attention is just presence. The grounding data last — not extracted, not surveyed, but accumulated through time spent in the room where the work actually happens.

What the practitioner is listening for is not problems. Problems are named later, and only after the community has had time to name them first. What the practitioner is listening for is what already works — what names the community has already given to its own practices that carry genuine weight. These names are the foundation everything else is built on. The practitioner's first job is to find them before anyone has told them to go looking.

This chapter is about what turns a contract into a journey. The contract names a deliverable and a fee. The journey is what happens when the practitioner has sat at enough kitchen tables that the community trusts them to hold the vocabulary correctly — not because the contract says so, but because the fish story was received with the respect it deserved.

The host is the door. Everything that follows is what happens after the practitioner walks through it and stays.

## PT3.3 — We don't hide our numbers; we deliver on them

The first document the practitioner produces is not a proposal. It is a set of numbers.

Not projections. Not assumptions. Not a best-case scenario designed to make the engagement look attractive. Numbers — the margin per unit, the volume required to reach viability, the cost of the equipment the plan depends on, the salary the operator will need to live while the institution gets on its feet. Dollar-honest, before anything else is honest.

This is not a posture of modesty. It is a structural decision. The community that receives a proposal before it receives the numbers will spend the engagement comparing the proposal to hope. The community that receives the numbers first will spend the engagement comparing the proposal to reality. The second community is building something that lasts.

We don't hide our numbers. We deliver on them.

Delivering on numbers is different from promising numbers. A promise lives in the future; a delivery lives in the record. The practitioner's credibility is not built by the quality of the forecast — it is built by the accuracy of the account of what actually happened and why, delivered promptly, in plain language, to everyone in the room who needs to know.

This is the dollar-honest register applied to the field. The same discipline that names the Jar Kitchen correctly in the vocabulary map names the shortfall correctly in the monthly report. The rename test runs on numbers too. *Net operating loss* is the vendor's word for *we spent more than we made this month and here is exactly why*. The community deserves the second sentence.

The boundary that holds the community's language is set in hempcrete. Not drywall. Not paint. Hempcrete — the material that cures over time, that hardens with age and humidity, that draws carbon out of the air instead of adding to it. The boundary is not a locked gate. It is a wall whose integrity improves the longer it is maintained.

And like hempcrete, it requires regular inspection.

The practitioner does not set the vocabulary boundary and leave. The inspection is built into the engagement from the start. The same monthly report that delivers the numbers also checks whether the words used to describe them are still carrying the meaning the community put into them, or whether the language has begun to drift toward the vendor's data model, the funder's framework, the government form. Drift is not dramatic. It is slow. It looks like a reasonable substitution. The inspection catches it before the compound interest of a wrong name runs for another quarter.

Goodwill compounds. That is the discipline's core economic claim, applied to a community institution. A community whose vocabulary holds — whose Jar Kitchen is still called the Jar Kitchen, whose Harvest Hold is still named by the people who use it, whose board can explain the 84¢ figure without referring to a consultant — that community has built something whose value increases with each generation that inherits it. The language is the asset. The regular inspection is the maintenance schedule.

## PT3.4 — The feast

The transition is not a handover. It is not a delivery of binders and a final invoice. It is not the practitioner stepping back from the org chart and the community stepping in.

It is a moment when the community solidifies its own voice. And that moment is celebrated.

There is a difference between a community that has been given a system and a community that has claimed one. The given system requires the donor to maintain goodwill with the recipient; the claimed system requires the community to maintain goodwill with itself. The practitioner's job — from the first kitchen table visit to the final meeting — is to make sure it is the second kind.

When it is the second kind, the transition is a binding experience. Not a ceremony of appreciation for the consultant who did the work. A recognition, by the community itself, that something has changed in how they hold their own institutions. The vocabulary is theirs. The books are theirs. The methodology is documented in words they wrote. The gate is set in hempcrete and the inspection schedule is maintained by people they trained, on timelines they control.

That community is worth defending.

Not because it is valuable to an outside observer. Because the people inside it know what they have built and what it cost them to build it. They know the fish story that started it. They know who sat at whose kitchen table. They know which numbers were delivered honestly and which quarter was the hardest. They know the word that was almost lost to a vendor's data model and was caught at the inspection and brought back.

Invaders — economic, linguistic, institutional — would have to get through the gate first. The gate is inspected regularly. It has been inspected regularly since before the invaders arrived. It will be inspected regularly after they have moved on.

So there will be a feast. There will be stories — the ones from before the engagement began, the ones from the middle when the numbers were hard, the ones from the moment the community spoke its own vocabulary back to the practitioner and it carried weight. There will be food that someone caught and someone preserved and someone cooked, and the story of how it got to the table will be told without a consultant's permission.

There will be healing. Not as a side effect of the work. As the thing the work was always pointing toward. There is healing that only a correct name can convey at the right moment. The moment when the community calls its institution by the name it chose, in the voice it trained, in the presence of everyone who helped build it — that moment carries a kind of repair that no proposal ever promised and no grant application ever captured.

That moment will be seized. The practitioner prepares for it from the first visit. The whole arc — Zone 0 practice, the host's kitchen table, the dollar-honest numbers, the hempcrete boundary inspected and held — is preparation for that moment. Not for the deliverable. Not for the invoice. For the feast.
