# Headwaters thesis context

Context for any deck, curriculum, proposal, or chat being built on Headwaters' work. This is the foundation everything else rests on — read it before writing or revising.

## Who Headwaters is

Headwaters is the agency. It publishes Headwaters Books (the internal accounting / ledger app), holds the Northern Food Systems Research Library, and is delivering the practitioner operating plan to Deer Lake First Nation. The practitioner is one person with software, training, and northern food-systems experience.

(Bright Side is a separate Rec Therapy app for long-term care — not part of this work. Don't reference it.)

## The thesis everything rests on

The western economic model is *slab construction*. It assumes a foundation already exists — concrete poured, utilities run, regulation set, banks lending, supply chains working, workforce showing up Monday to Friday. The slab is the social infrastructure that makes "small business" a sensible unit of analysis.

The North is *grassland*. The foundation is real; it's a different one. Land that flexes with the season. Workforce that flexes with the cycle. Demand that's lumpy. Capital that doesn't flow on western terms. Roads that come and go with the ice. Authority that lives in relationships, not regulation.

Neither is worse. They are different foundations, and the design rules are different.

The colonial mistake — and Headwaters owns its share of "we" in this — is to look at grassland and see *failed slab*. To say "the workforce isn't reliable enough" instead of "we designed the work for the wrong rhythm." The honest claim Headwaters makes:

> Northern reserves need infrastructure as materially good as anything in the south, built on the foundation that's actually there, with materials and methods that fit it. Not less. Different.

Guard against the romanticized misuse: "grassland-native" is not "grassland-lite." The store has to actually run. The food has to actually arrive. The books have to actually balance. Grassland design has to be as load-bearing as slab design, achieved through different shapes.

## What Headwaters is selling

Headwaters is a product company, not a services agency. Three revenue layers:

1. Software built by the practitioner, owned by the band, reused across bands.
2. Tech stack at markup — Headwaters integrates and resells the underlying tools (POS, accounting, comms, logistics) that the practitioner has tested in northern conditions.
3. Training programs that teach band staff how to run the system on their own rhythm, without imported labour.

Operations are *not* Headwaters' job at Deer Lake. The dad's company handles to/from logistics. The store is staffed by band members. Headwaters provides the operating system, the transparency layer, and monthly on-site training.

## The role roster (lean, not the seven-role team in earlier drafts)

- Dryden Hub Operator (formerly "Food Handler") — based at the Dryden shop, not Deer Lake. Earns across multiple revenue streams: salt production, kitchen piecework for other clients, ordering/distribution coordination for Deer Lake, phone.
- Bookkeeper, part-time, remote — kept because the practitioner cannot be in deep development mode and do CRA filings simultaneously.
- Technical advisor on retainer — small monthly hours, reviews architecture and money-touching code. Real hedge against solo-developer risk.
- Training & curriculum partner — likely an Indigenous educator partner whose voice fits "the answer should speak to us."
- Casual local pod in Deer Lake — paid by the job for batch days and market tables. Local employment line, no embed.
- Practitioner — software, training delivery, monthly visits, advisory.

Roles deliberately removed from earlier drafts: Operations Manager (absorbed into Dryden Hub Operator), embedded Food Handler at Deer Lake (band staffs their own store), IT/Tech as a separate hire (practitioner is this, with the technical advisor as a check). The practitioner's personal-capital roles (housecleaner, tutor, handyman) move off the contract budget into personal income.

## The design problem the product is built around

"What happens when people don't show up?" — common in northern reserves, normally answered by importing outside reliable labour, which creates the dependency loops the band is trying to exit.

The grassland-native answer designs the system to *assume* people don't always show up, and makes the store run anyway. Design directions to draw on:

- Shift-as-a-market (claim the next shift, don't be obligated to it)
- Pods, not roles (every function owned by 2–4 people)
- Cross-training as baseline (whoever shows up can do whatever needs doing)
- Software that runs the store when nobody's there (automated reorder, reconciliation, dashboards)
- Calendar built around community life (hunting, goose camp, funerals, ice road season)
- Accountability through visibility, not punishment (the data does the talking, not the practitioner)

The training curriculum teaches all of these as a coherent practice — and it's directly sellable to other northern band stores.

## What Deer Lake gains

Sovereignty (they run their own store). Cultural fit (the system is designed for their rhythm, not against it). A much smaller contract size (probably half of earlier drafts). Transparency across every relationship — Headwaters, dad's company, suppliers, contractor.

What Deer Lake gives up: an on-call practitioner doing operations. They have to be more responsible for their day-to-day. That's a feature, not a bug — but be honest about it.

## Naming rule

All slide, section, page, and document names use words only. No numbers, no letters, no initials, no Roman numerals. Editorial naming, not versioned naming.
